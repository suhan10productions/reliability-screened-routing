\begin{abstract}
Routes planned on nominal travel times run late under congestion and random
delay. Padding every trip, the usual remedy, adds distance and vehicles even to
safe routes. This paper adds a statistical reliability check to
any solver for the vehicle routing problem with time windows. The solver produces candidate plans with internal buffers,
leaving customer windows unchanged. A plan is admitted only if a 95\% Wilson lower
bound on the probability that at least 95\% of customers are served on time
reaches 0.95, and is then tested on fresh scenarios. Capping each buffer at the
customer's direct-reachability limit removes a failure that budgeted-robust
models share. On synthetic instances with 20--200 customers, screening beat 20\%
padding by \ProcConsRange{} percentage points, though not significantly at 200
customers. In three timestamped protocols, seven of eight primary hypotheses
held; the exception was a conservative-speed fallback. Results depend on
the assumed delay model.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{vehicle routing; time windows; stochastic travel times; time-dependent
travel times; chance constraints; simulation; reproducibility}

\section{Introduction}

In the vehicle routing problem with time windows (VRPTW), a fleet of vehicles
with limited capacity and shift length must serve each customer within an
agreed window \citep{solomon1987,toth2014}. Most planning tools solve it with a
single matrix of travel times. On the road, travel times change through the day
and from one day to the next, and a delay early in a route pushes every later
customer towards the end of its window. A plan that is optimal on nominal times
takes no account of this.

The simplest safeguard is to plan with a lower speed than expected. Padding every trip by a fixed percentage is easy to explain and needs no
new software. Its cost is that off-peak trips are padded as much as peak ones,
so the plan uses extra distance and vehicles whether a route is at risk or not.
Stochastic and robust formulations of the VRPTW spend that protection more
carefully, because they model the uncertainty inside the optimization
\citep{errico2018,agra2013,gendreau2016}. They need a solver written for their
uncertainty model, however, and most operators run a general-purpose solver
that they cannot easily replace.

This paper studies a procedure that keeps the operator's solver and adds a
statistical test after it. The solver is asked for several candidate plans,
each planned against internal deadlines that have been brought forward by a
buffer, and the windows offered to customers do not change. Each candidate is
simulated on a set of days drawn from a time-dependent model with correlated
delays. It is admitted only if a one-sided Wilson lower bound on the
probability of a good service day reaches a target, and the best admitted plan
under the planner's own objective is returned. The reliability of that plan is
then reported from a second, independent set of simulated days that played no
part in choosing it. Because the test comes after the solve, the same rule
applies to any solver and any objective.

The paper contributes in three ways.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It sets out the screening-and-validation procedure and identifies a
failure that internal buffers can cause. When a deadline is brought forward far
enough, a customer can become unreachable even by a direct trip from the depot,
and the solver then returns no plan at all. Capping each customer's buffer at
its direct-reachability limit prevents this. The same failure occurs in a
budgeted-robust VRPTW model, and the corresponding relief prevents it there as
well.
\item It compares screening with uniform padding on matched instances and
reports what the extra reliability costs. In a development study on 53 synthetic
instances with 20 to 200 customers, the screened procedure was \ProcConsRange{}
percentage points more reliable than 20\% padding on the 46 instances where
padding found a plan, depending on size, although the interval at 200 customers
included zero. Three protocols, each timestamped
in a public repository with frozen code before any of its runs
\citep{ali2026freeze,ali2026freeze2,ali2026freeze3}, then tested the main
findings on new instances with two generators, OR-Tools \citep{ortools} and
hybrid genetic search (HGS; \citealp{vidal2022,wouda2024}). Seven of the eight
primary hypotheses met their decision criteria, and the one that did not is
reported alongside the others.
\item It sets the procedure against a budgeted-robust optimizer
\citep{bertsimas2004,agra2013} and tests a rule for instances on which no plan
passes. Robust plans with reachability relief, put through the same screen, were
more reliable on instances with up to 100 customers. They also used more
vehicles, and when they were limited to the comparator's fleet their advantage
was no longer statistically detectable. Returning the best-screened candidate when nothing
is admitted raised the average service over all instances in a prespecified
test on 53 further instances.
\end{enumerate}

All instances are synthetic and the delay model is assumed, so the evidence is
about repeatability in one controlled setting. Every number in the paper can be
regenerated from the archived records. The full model, all result tables and
the further checks are in the Supporting Information (SI).

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:method}
defines the models and the procedure. Sections~\ref{sec:design}
and~\ref{sec:results} give the design and the results,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses practical use and limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Time-dependent travel needs some care, because applying a speed multiplier at
the moment of departure can break the first-in-first-out (FIFO) property. The
integrated speed profile of \citet{ichoua2003}, which is used here, avoids this,
and \citet{ghiani2014} analyse its properties. For uncertain travel and service
times, the stochastic VRPTW literature uses chance constraints, recourse models,
approximate feasibility tests and data-driven formulations
\citep{laporte1992,kenyon2003,li2010,errico2018,bomboi2022,serrano2026}.
\citet{ehmke2015} build per-customer service levels into a routing method,
\citet{tas2014} model time dependence, stochastic travel and soft windows
together, and \citet{bomboi2022} check route feasibility when travel times are
correlated and time-dependent.

The closest work to the buffers used here is on self-imposed time windows,
introduced by \citet{jabali2015}, and on window assignment under stochastic
travel times with per-customer chance constraints \citep{vareias2019}. Both
place the buffers inside a dedicated optimization and judge reliability one
customer at a time. Neither can be attached to an unmodified solver, and neither
bounds the probability that a whole service day goes well.

Robust models require the plan to stay feasible for every realization in an
uncertainty set. \citet{agra2013} solve a robust VRPTW with the budgeted set of
\citet{bertsimas2004}, \citet{jaillet2016} minimize a requirements violation
index, and \citet{zhang2019} develop a riskiness index together with an exact
algorithm. \citet{gendreau1996} and \citet{gendreau2016} survey stochastic
routing more broadly. The experiments below compare the procedure with padding
and with one budgeted robust model. Chance-constrained and distributionally
robust VRPTW solvers are not included.

The procedure studied here makes its statistical claim after the solve and
builds no feasibility approximation into a specialist search. Routes come from a replaceable nominal solver, a family of internal buffers is searched
on one set of simulated days, and the chosen plan is checked once on a held-out
set. The service event limits the share of late customers over the whole day,
which is closer to the plan-level risk measures of \citet{kenyon2003} than to
per-customer constraints, and the windows offered to customers never change.
Monte Carlo approximations of chance constraints need a separate check of the
solution they select \citep{ahmed2008}, and the held-out set provides it. The
Wilson bound is used because normal-approximation intervals behave poorly for
probabilities close to one \citep{wilson1927,brown2001}.

\section{Model and procedure}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Planning model}

Let $N=\{0,1,\ldots,n\}$ be the nodes, with depot 0 and customers $C$, and let
$V$ be a homogeneous fleet with capacity $Q$ and maximum shift duration
$H^{\max}$. Customer $i$ has demand $\delta_i$, service time $s_i$ and an
offered window $[l_i,u_i]$ for the start of service. Planning uses nominal
distances $d_{ij}$ and travel times $\bar t_{ij}$. A plan assigns customers to
routes, fixes the order of visits and sets a depot departure $d_v$ and return
$r_v$ for each vehicle $v$, with service starting at time $a_i$ at customer $i$.
The full formulation is in \SI{sec:si-model}, where $x_{ijv}=1$ if vehicle $v$
drives arc $(i,j)$ and $y_v=1$ if vehicle $v$ is used. A plan is described by
four criteria:
\begin{equation}
 F_1=c_f\sum_{v}\sum_{i,j}d_{ij}x_{ijv},\quad
 F_2=\sum_{i\in C}\max\{0,s^*-(u_i-a_i)\},\quad
 F_3=\sum_{v}(r_v-d_v),\quad
 F_4=\sum_{v}y_v.                                       \label{eq:criteria}
\end{equation}
These are the distance cost at $c_f$ per kilometre, the slack deficit against a
target slack $s^*$, the total driver duration and the number of vehicles. Each
criterion is scaled by its range $[L_k,U_k]$ over four anchor solutions, each of
which minimizes one criterion alone, and the candidate objective is
\begin{equation}
 Z=\sum_{k=1}^{4}w_k\,\frac{F_k-L_k}{\max\{U_k-L_k,1\}}.        \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The weights $\mathbf{w}=(0.5693,0.2643,0.1055,0.0609)$ were fixed before any
experiment and are treated as an experimental setting. Their origin is given in
\SI{sec:ahp-matrix}, and their effect is examined in Section~\ref{sec:checks}.
The objective shapes the candidates and ranks the admitted ones. The admission
test does not use it, so any other scalar objective could take its place.

\subsection{Evaluation model}

The day is divided into periods $[T_k,T_{k+1})$ with speeds $\nu_k>0$. When a
vehicle leaves $i$ for $j$ at time $\tau$, the deterministic travel time is the
smallest $\Delta\geq0$ with
\begin{equation}
 \int_{\tau}^{\tau+\Delta}\nu(u)\,du=d_{ij},                 \label{eq:fifo}
\end{equation}
which preserves FIFO \citep{ichoua2003}. In scenario $\omega$ this time is
multiplied by $\exp\{\varepsilon_{ij}(\omega)\}$, where
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_{ij}(\omega)=\sigma\bigl(\sqrt{\rho}\,\xi_0(\omega)
 +\sqrt{1-\rho}\,\xi_{ij}(\omega)\bigr),                       \label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
$\xi_0$ is a standard normal factor shared by all arcs in the scenario and
$\xi_{ij}$ is specific to the arc. The multiplier has median one, so the
deterministic profile gives the median travel time. A fixed set of simulated
days drawn in this way is called a scenario \emph{bank}. Each arc draws from its
own random stream, keyed by the bank seed and its endpoints, so plans evaluated
on the same bank meet the same delays on the arcs they share. In every scenario
each vehicle leaves the depot at its planned time.

\subsection{Screening and validation}

For plan $R$ and scenario $\omega$, let $q(R,\omega)$ be the share of customers
whose service starts by $u_i$. The service event is
$J(R,\omega)=\mathbf{1}\{q(R,\omega)\geq\kappa\}$ with $\kappa=0.95$, so a
day with $J=1$ is a success, and the target is $\Pr[J=1]\geq\alpha$ with
$\alpha=0.95$.
Asking for every customer to be on time would make a poor target on large
instances, since that probability falls with the number of customers even for
good plans. It is reported, but it is not used for admission.

For each buffer $\beta$ in the grid, the solver plans against the earlier
deadline $\max\{l_i,u_i-\beta\}$ and minimizes~\eqref{eq:objective}; the anchor
ranges stay at their uncontracted values. Each candidate is simulated on a
screening bank of $N_s$ scenarios. If it succeeds on a share $\hat p$ of them,
its one-sided Wilson lower bound at level $1-\eta$ is
\begin{equation}
 L(\hat p)=\frac{\hat p+z^2/(2N_s)-z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/N_s+z^2/(4N_s^2)}}{1+z^2/N_s},
 \qquad z=z_{1-\eta}.                                          \label{eq:wilson}
\end{equation}
A candidate is admitted when $L(\hat p)\geq\alpha$, and the admitted candidate
with the smallest $Z$ is selected. The selected plan is simulated once more on a
larger validation bank that has not been used before, and this run only reports
its reliability. Lateness is always scored against the offered windows $u_i$.
If no candidate is admitted, a fallback rule fixed in advance supplies a plan,
so the procedure returns something on every instance. The results therefore
report the validated reliability of admitted plans separately from the
reliability of whatever the procedure returned, fallbacks included. The average
of the latter over instances is called \emph{pooled service}. The Wilson
bound applies to each candidate on its own, and a family-wise version over the
whole grid is examined in Section~\ref{sec:checks}. The procedure has four
steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For each buffer $\beta\in\{0,5,\ldots,60\}$ minutes, solve the planning
model with deadlines $\max\{l_i,u_i-\beta\}$ (or~\eqref{eq:cap} when capped).
\item Simulate each candidate on the screening bank and compute $L(\hat p)$.
\item If some candidate has $L(\hat p)\geq\alpha$, select the admitted
candidate with the smallest $Z$; otherwise apply the fallback rule.
\item Simulate the selected plan once on the validation bank and report its
service probability.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Reachability-capped contraction}

If a deadline is brought forward too far, a customer can become unreachable even
by a direct trip from the depot, and then no plan exists at that buffer. Let
$e_i=\max\{l_i,\bar t_{0i}\}$ be the earliest time at which service could start
after a direct trip. The capped procedure plans against
\begin{equation}
 u_i^{\rm cap}(\beta)=\min\bigl\{u_i,\max\{l_i,u_i-\beta,e_i\}\bigr\},
 \label{eq:cap}
\end{equation}
so contraction alone never makes a reachable customer unreachable. The cap does
not guarantee that a joint routing exists or that the target will be met. A
shortest-path relaxation certifies when a contracted model is infeasible, which
separates proven infeasibility from search failure (\SI{sec:capping}).

\subsection{Comparators}

\emph{Conservative speed} minimizes distance at 33.3~km/h instead of 40~km/h,
which pads every trip by 20\%. \emph{Screened conservative speed} applies the
same screen to candidates planned at that speed. The \emph{original procedure}
screens contracted candidates without the cap, and the \emph{capped procedure}
uses~\eqref{eq:cap}. Candidates come from OR-Tools \citep{ortools} or from the
HGS of PyVRP \citep{wouda2024}. HGS solves the same planning model, but it
cannot include the slack-deficit term while it generates plans.

The robust comparator is a VRPTW with budgeted uncertainty
\citep{bertsimas2004,agra2013}. The travel time of each arc may rise from
$\bar t_{ij}$ to $\bar t_{ij}+\lceil\theta\bar t_{ij}\rceil$, and a route must
remain feasible whenever at most $\Gamma$ of its arcs take their upper value.
Under \emph{box uncertainty} all of them may. Feasibility is checked exactly with
the dynamic programme of \citet{agra2013} inside a large neighbourhood search
written for this study. The search minimizes distance and stops after a fixed
number of iterations, so its output is deterministic. Robust models run into
the same reachability problem as contraction, since a customer that cannot be
reached in time by a direct trip at its upper travel time makes every plan
infeasible. The \emph{relieved} variant raises that customer's robust deadline to
its worst-case direct start, while every original window must still hold under
nominal travel times. \emph{Screened robust} passes all robust plans for an
instance through the same screen. It returns the shortest admitted plan, or the
plan with the highest screening success if none is admitted.
\SI{sec:robust-design} gives the details.

Three fallback rules are compared. The \emph{slack-aware} rule returns the
uncontracted plan of the weighted objective, the \emph{conservative} rule
returns the generator's conservative-speed plan, and the \emph{best-screened}
rule returns the candidate of the procedure's own family with the highest
screening success. None of them looks at a validation result. Unless stated
otherwise, the OR-Tools procedures use the slack-aware fallback and the HGS
procedures the conservative one, as their protocols specify.

\section{Experimental design}\label{sec:design}

Each instance has a central depot and customers spread uniformly over a
$100\times100$ grid. Demands are uniform on 10--49 units, service takes 10
minutes, window widths are uniform on 90--149 minutes, vehicles carry 1000 units
and the shift lasts 600 minutes from 06:00. Table~\ref{tab:parameters} lists the
instance sizes and the model constants. Speeds drop during a morning peak from
07:00 to 09:00, and the speed profile continues into the evening so that routes
running past the end of the shift are still evaluated.

In the development study every configuration was run on 53 seeded instances,
and its findings are treated as exploratory. All configurations of an instance
share the same screening and validation banks, so every comparison is paired.
Intervals across instances come from a paired bootstrap with 20,000 resamples
and a fixed seed. A configuration that finds no plan scores zero on that
instance, so a failure counts against the configuration that failed. Software
errors are logged and stop the analysis, and they are never scored as routing
outcomes.

Three confirmatory protocols followed (Table~\ref{tab:studies}). Each protocol fixed
its hypotheses, tests, decision rules, failure policy and instance seeds. It was
committed to a public repository, together with a \mbox{SHA-256} manifest of the code
that runs and analyses it, and archived on Zenodo
\citep{ali2026freeze,ali2026freeze2,ali2026freeze3}, all before any of its
instances was solved.
The analysis programs refuse to run unless every record is present and every
frozen file matches its manifest. Protocol~1 tested the OR-Tools procedures on
53 new instances. Protocol~2 used 53 further instances to test HGS as the
generator and the conservative fallback. Protocol~3 had two parts: part A
repeated the robust comparison on the \PaInstances{} earlier confirmatory
instances with up to 100 customers, and part B tested the best-screened fallback
on 53 new instances. Within each study the two primary hypotheses use 97.5\%
intervals (a Bonferroni adjustment), and each is confirmed only if its interval
excludes zero in the stated direction. Secondary hypotheses use 95\% intervals
and cannot rescue a primary one. Since every instance comes from the same
generator and delay model, the protocols test whether the findings repeat in
this setting; external validity is outside their scope. Hardware, software
versions and the course of each run are given in
Sections~\ref{S-sec:confdesign}, \ref{S-sec:conf2design}
and~\ref{S-sec:conf3design}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Screening against padding}

Table~\ref{tab:paired} gives the paired differences from conservative speed on
the 46 development instances where conservative speed found a plan. The complete
original procedure was \ProcConsRange{} points more reliable, depending on size,
although the interval at 200 customers includes zero. The slack-aware objective
on its own, without screening, was less reliable than padding at every size, so
the gain needs both the family of buffered candidates and the screen. Applying
the same screen to conservative-speed candidates also helped, which makes a
fixed 20\% pad the weaker of the two padding baselines.

The advantage grows with noise when delays are independent
(Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}). For fixed plans with $\rho=0$, the difference
between the original procedure and conservative speed went from
$\SweepLowIndep$ points at $\sigma=0.10$, where padding was slightly ahead, to
\SweepHighIndep{} points at $\sigma=0.30$. With strongly correlated delays the
increase was not monotonic. Depending on size, the original procedure drove
\ItorCostDistRange\% more distance than conservative speed, and its fleet was
sometimes larger and sometimes smaller (Table~\ref{S-tab:cost} of the SI).

\subsection{Capping the contraction}

On \FailureCount{} development instances the original procedure admitted no
plan, and on \CertifiedFailures{} of them a reachability obstruction was
certified at some buffer level. Capping (Table~\ref{tab:screen}) raised the
number of admitted instances from \OriginalSelected{} to \CappedSelected{} and
pooled service by \CappedGainInterval{} points. At 100 and 200 customers the
capped procedure admitted a plan on \CappedLargeSelected{} instances and pooled
\CappedLargeRange\%. On average it used \CappedDistanceCost\% more distance and
\CappedFleetCost{} more vehicles than the original procedure, and
\CappedFleetLarge{} more vehicles at 200 customers.

\subsection{Comparison with robust optimization}

On the \RobInstances{} development instances with up to 100 customers, the
standard robust model under box uncertainty with $\theta=0.6$ had no plan on
\RobNoPlanBoxSix{} instances. In each case some customer could not be protected
even on a direct trip, and every smaller budget failed on the same instances.
With relief every setting had a plan, and moving between settings traded
distance for service along a smooth curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}). Screened
robust with relief pooled \RobScreenedCapP\%, compared with \RobOrtCappedP\% for
the OR-Tools capped procedure and \RobHgsCappedP\% for the HGS one, and it used
\RobScreenedCapMinusHgsFleet{} more vehicles than the HGS procedure.

Part A of protocol~3 confirmed these findings on the earlier confirmatory
instances with up to 100 customers. Screened robust with relief beat the
OR-Tools capped procedure by \PaROne{} points over all \PaInstances{} of them,
and the HGS capped procedure by \PaRTwo{} points over the 45 instances of
protocol~2, the only ones with HGS plans. The two estimates happen to coincide.
Screened robust with relief pooled \PaScreenedP\% and used \PaRThree{} more
vehicles than the HGS procedure. Within the robust family, screening returned
plans \PaRFourAbs\% shorter than the most protective setting, at a cost of
\PaBoxMinusScr{} points of service. When the robust plans were limited after the
fact to no more vehicles than the comparator used, they were no longer
measurably more reliable. The differences were \FmConfHgs{} [\FmConfHgsLo,
\FmConfHgsHi] points against the HGS capped procedure and \FmConfOrt{}
[\FmConfOrtLo, \FmConfOrtHi] against the OR-Tools one. On these instances the
screened procedures used fewer vehicles at some cost in reliability. Results with
200 customers are in \SI{sec:robust}.

\subsection{Fallback rule}

After the first two protocols, the stored records showed that returning the
best-screened candidate when nothing is admitted would have raised pooled
service in every study and for every procedure, by \FbGainMin{} to
\FbGainMax{} points. Part B of protocol~3 tested this rule on \PbInstances{} new
instances. Compared with each procedure's default fallback, it improved the
OR-Tools capped procedure by \PbFOne{} points and the HGS capped procedure by
\PbFTwo{}, bringing pooled service to \PbFThree\% and \PbFFour\%. Both estimates
are just below the 95\% target. On \PbNoAnchor{} of the \PbInstances{} instances
the anchor solves found no plan, so neither procedure could run and that
instance scored zero.
Without it, pooled service was \PbExOrt\% and \PbExHgs\% (descriptive). Plans
returned by a fallback have not passed the screen.

\subsection{Sensitivity and further checks}\label{sec:checks}

\emph{Objective weights.} With equal weights, fewer development instances were
admitted (\WtEqualAdmitted{} against \WtBaseAdmitted{} in the same run), and the
advantage over conservative speed fell to \WtEqualPCMean{} points
[\WtEqualPCLo, \WtEqualPCHi]. A later run that repeated both weight vectors
found the advantage falling from \BrOrigAdv{} to \BrEqAdv{} points
[\BrEqAdvLo, \BrEqAdvHi]. Raising only the driver-duration weight changed
service by \BrDurDiff{} points, and raising only the fleet weight changed it by
\BrFleetDiff{}, and the run cannot separate the two effects. The stored
candidates suggest that the advantage depends on how much waiting time the
objective leaves in the routes to absorb delays. None of the protocols varied
the weights (\SI{sec:weights}).

\emph{Benchmark instances.} On \SolomonN{} Solomon R1 and RC1 instances,
OR-Tools found a plan for \SolomonPlans{} and screening admitted
\SolomonAdmitted{}. On 20 Gehring--Homberger instances with 200 customers
\citep{gehring1999}, with HGS as the generator, the procedure could run on
\GhAttempted{}, admitted a plan on \GhAdmitted{} and beat conservative speed by
\GhDiff{} points [\GhDiffLo, \GhDiffHi] (Sections~\ref{S-sec:solomon}
and~\ref{S-sec:gh}).

\emph{Delay-model misspecification.} Under four alternative delay models, the
fixed plans of the capped procedures lost up to \MisCappedDrop{} points, and
they stayed ahead of conservative speed. When the stored candidates were
screened again under each model and the best-screened fallback was used, pooled
service stayed between \ResLoOrt{} and \ResHiHgs\% (\SI{sec:misspec}).

\emph{Family-wise screening and computation time.} Across all studies and
procedures, a Bonferroni bound over the 13 buffer levels removed all
\FwMissOrig{} of the \FwAdmittedOrig{} admitted plans that later had a validation
lower bound below 0.95, while \FwMissFw{} of the newly selected plans did. The cost
was at most \FwLostMax{} admissions per study and procedure, and pooled service
changed by between \FwPooledChangeMin{} and \FwPooledChangeMax{} points.
Screening a candidate took \TimeScreenTwenty{} to \TimeScreenTwoHundred{}~ms,
whereas generating one took seconds (Sections~\ref{S-sec:familywise}
and~\ref{S-sec:time}).

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

For a planner who already runs a VRPTW solver, the change is cheap. Generating a handful of buffered variants and simulating them costs
little beyond the solves themselves, and it needs no change to the solver or its
objective. On the same instances it gave plans that were measurably more
reliable than a fixed 20\% pad. Every admitted plan also comes with a tested
lower bound on a defined service event, and the validation bank shows
how the plan did on days it had not seen. The reachability cap is a one-line
change that removed most certified failures, and it should be applied to any method
that adds buffers, robust models included.

The evidence is limited in several ways. The instances are synthetic, and the
disturbance parameters are assumed and have not been estimated from vehicle GPS
data,
so the numbers should not be read as forecasts for a real fleet. The protocols
test repeatability with one instance generator and one delay model, and the
Solomon and Gehring--Homberger checks are small. The OR-Tools generator is weaker
than the state of the art, which is why protocol~2 repeated the test with HGS. Even so,
two generators cannot show that the result holds for every solver. The robust
comparison covers one budgeted model with one search configuration, and
distributionally robust and riskiness-index methods
\citep{jaillet2016,zhang2019} were not tested. The advantage depends on the
objective weights, and equal weights more than halved it. The Wilson bound holds
for each candidate separately and gives no simultaneous guarantee over the grid.
Finally, keeping every departure fixed makes the evaluation harsher than an
operator who adjusts departures would face. Breakdowns, new orders and
rolling-horizon replanning are left out, so the study says nothing about how to
recover once a delay has happened.

Under the prespecified fallback rules, pooled service of the OR-Tools and HGS
procedures stayed below 95\% in every study, because some instances had no
admitted plan. The best-screened fallback
narrows that gap, but closing it needs better candidates on those instances,
where a stronger or a stochastic generator could help most.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On matched synthetic instances, screening a family of buffered plans from a
standard VRPTW solver gave more reliable service than padding every trip, with
OR-Tools and with HGS, and three timestamped protocols supported the main
findings. One primary hypothesis was not confirmed. Returning the
conservative-speed plan when nothing is admitted did not measurably help the
capped OR-Tools procedure, although returning the best-screened candidate did.
Capping each customer's buffer at its direct-reachability limit removed a
failure that also prevents robust models from finding any plan. A
budgeted-robust optimizer with the same relief was more reliable still, but it
used more vehicles, and at the comparator's fleet size its advantage was not
statistically detectable. The screening layer and the reachability relief worked with every
planner tested. The study has yet to be repeated on a real road network with
delay parameters estimated from fleet data.
