\emph{International Journal of Systems Science: Operations \& Logistics}

Dear Editor,

I submit the manuscript \emph{Certified reliability for any routing solver: screening
buffered plans against travel-time uncertainty} for consideration as a research
article.

Delivery firms promise time windows, but most plan with fixed travel times and protect
themselves by padding every trip, which says nothing about how often the plan will be on
time. Methods that model the uncertainty directly need a solver built for that model. The
manuscript takes the systems view of a firm that keeps its existing solver and adds a
reliability layer around it: the solver produces plans with capped internal buffers, the
plans are screened by simulation, and the chosen plan is certified on independent
simulated days.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a reliability layer that works with an unchanged solver, with a certificate that, under
the modelled delays, holds for any solver and selection rule (Theorem 1). The guarantee
applies a standard a posteriori result from sampled chance-constrained programming; the
contribution is the layer and the evidence, not new statistical theory. Two further results
say when a certificate survives a wrong delay model: it carries over to any real delays no
worse than modelled (Proposition 1), and certifying against a set of models guarantees each
of them (Proposition 2);
\item evidence that this works: none of \CertTotal{} certified plans failed its
certificate on fresh days. Certificates under one model often failed under harsher delays;
certifying against five models removed every failure, at a cost the paper reports in full:
a plan was certified on \StOStress{} of \StOInst{} instances instead of \StOSingle{}, with
\StOFleet{} more vehicles where both screens selected one;
\item an ablation with the HGS solver showing that the gain comes from the buffers and the
screen, not from the planner's objective: capped buffers planned for distance alone and
screened were \AbDDiff{} points more reliable than 20\% padding, as reliable as the full
procedure, while the weighted plan without buffers or screening was less reliable than
padding;
\item fixed-order certification (Proposition 4), which lets a firm certify the cheapest
good plan instead of the screened one with the same guarantee, and a count of how many real
days of history a certificate without a delay model needs (\LfDaysZero{} at 95\%,
Proposition 3);
\item tests beyond synthetic data: benchmark instances; real Amazon delivery routes with
delays fitted to \NycTripsM{} million New York taxi trips, an illustration on real routes
with assumed windows; and real pickup operations with real windows and GPS tracks in five
Chinese cities, which show that dispatch records cannot serve as delay data while
travel and handling measured from GPS are stable over time.
\end{itemize}

The closest work is the robust data-driven routing of Zhang, Zhang, Lim and Sim
(\emph{Operations Research}, 2021), which also reduces to deadlines brought forward by a
buffer. Their bound holds over a Wasserstein ambiguity set inside a dedicated solver; ours
is a finite-sample certificate that needs no change to the solver. Simheuristics rank
solutions by simulated reliability within their own search; our certificate can be added
on top of any of them. These differences are set out in Section~2.

I believe the paper fits the journal's interest in operations research for logistics and
in reliability. The main claims were tested in three confirmatory studies whose hypotheses
were fixed and publicly timestamped before they were run, and all code, instances and
records are openly available. The limitations are stated plainly. On the real routes, with
the HGS solver, screening was more reliable than padding by \AmzHBothDiff{} and
\AmzHTBothDiff{} points under two of the three delay models, at the same number of
vehicles; under the mildest model padding was already reliable and slightly ahead.
